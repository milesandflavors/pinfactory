<p>Japan looks like one of the hardest trips to plan. It's not — if you keep the route simple and resist the urge to add every city you've ever heard of. This 10-day Japan itinerary covers Tokyo, Hakone, Kyoto, and Osaka: four stops that deliver on a first visit, with enough time in each place to actually settle in rather than just pass through.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="before-you-start">Sort This Before You Book Anything Else</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Japan Rail Pass reality check:</strong> there is no 10-day nationwide Japan Rail Pass. The official nationwide pass is sold for 7, 14, or 21 days, so for this route you need to compare individual train tickets against the current 7-day or 14-day pass price before buying. For a simple one-way Tokyo - Hakone - Kyoto - Osaka route, the pass is not an automatic win; it becomes more attractive if you add a return to Tokyo or extra long-distance JR day trips.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If you decide a JR Pass makes sense, buy through the official site or an authorized seller and check the current yen price before purchase. Also note that Nozomi and Mizuho shinkansen services have extra conditions for Japan Rail Pass holders, so always check the exact train type before assuming it is included.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="tokyo">Days 1–3 in Tokyo: Arriving, Adjusting, Exploring</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Tokyo is the world's largest metropolitan area, and it takes at least a day to stop feeling overwhelmed by it. Three nights is the right amount — enough to get past the disorientation and actually enjoy the city, not so much that you lose time in Kyoto.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:group {"style":{"color":{"background":"#F0F4F8"},"spacing":{"padding":{"top":"20px","bottom":"20px","left":"24px","right":"24px"}},"border":{"left":{"color":"#5B8DB8","width":"4px"}}}} -->

<p><strong>Reality check:</strong> Don't plan Day 1. Land, find your hotel, eat something within walking distance, sleep. Jet lag eastward from the US is brutal — fighting it on Day 1 costs you Day 2 and 3.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph --></div>

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="undefined-1">Day 1 — Shinjuku: Arrive and Orient</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Stay in Shinjuku for this itinerary — central, excellent subway connections in every direction, and the neighborhood itself gives you a complete first impression of Tokyo without going anywhere. No agenda on Day 1. Walk around. Find dinner nearby. The neon, the food stalls, the general motion of the place is worth experiencing without a plan attached to it.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="undefined-2">Day 2 — Asakusa and the Historic East Side</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Go early. This applies everywhere in Japan, but especially here.</strong> Senso-ji temple before 8am is quiet and atmospheric. By 10am it's crowded. By noon it's genuinely difficult to move through the approach street.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Senso-ji temple</strong> — arrive before 8am, the precinct is a different place at that hour</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Nakamise shopping street</strong> — traditional crafts, snacks, souvenirs; better browsed early or late</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Ueno Park and Tokyo National Museum</strong> — 10-minute walk from Asakusa, good for the afternoon</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Asakusa backstreets</strong> — the lanes behind the temple are more interesting than the main approach; most people miss them entirely</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="undefined-3">Day 3 — Harajuku, Meiji Shrine, Shibuya</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The west side of Tokyo is completely different in character from Asakusa — younger, more fashion-forward, more international. A full contrast day.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Meiji Shrine</strong> (morning) — 30 minutes of quiet forested paths in the middle of the city; adjacent to Harajuku and a good first stop</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Harajuku / Takeshita Street</strong> — worth seeing for the spectacle, even if it's not your shopping destination</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Omotesando</strong> — Tokyo's answer to a Parisian boulevard; good lunch options</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Shibuya Crossing</strong> (5–7pm) — go at rush hour for the full effect; the synchronized crossing of a thousand people is worth seeing in person</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Shibuya Sky observation deck</strong> - check current ticket prices and time slots; skip it if the street-level crossing view is enough for you</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="hakone">Day 4 in Hakone: Mount Fuji Views and a Ryokan Night</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Hakone is the reset the trip needs. After three days in Tokyo, one night in a traditional ryokan with an outdoor onsen recalibrates everything. It's also the best chance on this route to see Mount Fuji — though "chance" is the right word. Visibility is unpredictable. Roughly half of visitors don't get a clear view. Go for the ryokan and treat Fuji as a bonus.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- /wp:image -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Getting there:</strong> From Shinjuku, the Romancecar train runs directly to Hakone-Yumoto in ~85 minutes, no transfers. With the JR Pass, take the standard JR line to Odawara and connect to the Hakone Tozan railway.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>The Hakone Round Course</strong> — do this in the morning:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Hakone ropeway</strong> — over the Owakudani volcanic crater, best Fuji views when clear</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Lake Ashi ferry</strong> — across the lake with Fuji in the background (on clear days)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Hakone Open Air Museum</strong> — modern sculpture in an outdoor setting; better than it sounds, genuinely worth 2 hours</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>The ryokan:</strong> This is the one booking to make early. A ryokan with dinner and breakfast included is often one of the most expensive nights of the trip, but it gives you the full Hakone experience: tatami room, futon bedding, multi-course kaiseki dinner, and onsen bathing. Compare current rates and cancellation terms before you lock it in.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="kyoto">Days 5–7 in Kyoto: The City That Makes Japan Worth the Flight</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Kyoto is the reason most people decide to go to Japan. More UNESCO World Heritage Sites than almost anywhere else on earth — and unlike most places on that list, they're consistently excellent. Three nights is the minimum. Two is not enough.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Getting there from Hakone:</strong> Check out in the morning, train to Odawara, shinkansen to Kyoto. Door to door: <strong>about 2.5 hours</strong>. You arrive mid-afternoon with the rest of Day 5 ahead of you.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="undefined-4">Day 5 — Arashiyama: Bamboo, Temples, and the Right Start</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The bamboo grove at 7am and the bamboo grove at 11am are essentially different experiences. Go early — this is the most useful piece of advice for the entire Kyoto portion.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:image {"id":1716,"sizeSlug":"large","linkDestination":"none"} -->
<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img alt="Arashiyama bamboo grove in Kyoto photographed early in the morning" src="/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/atsushi-tsubokura-5oguG6eOYrk-unsplash-1-1024x681.jpg" class="wp-image-1716"/><figcaption class="wp-element-caption">Arashiyama bamboo grove before 8am — one of the most atmospheric sights in Japan, best experienced at dawn</figcaption></figure>
<!-- /wp:image -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Arashiyama bamboo grove</strong> — before 8am; by 10am the tour groups arrive and the effect is gone</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Tenryu-ji temple</strong> — UNESCO-listed garden, walk from the bamboo grove, worth 45 minutes</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Togetsukyo Bridge</strong> — the classic Arashiyama view over the Oi River</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Jojakko-ji and Nison-in temples</strong> — the quieter back-trail most visitors skip because they leave after the bamboo; don't skip it</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Iwatayama Monkey Park</strong> (afternoon) — 20-minute uphill walk, free-roaming macaques, city view; a genuine experience rather than a tourist attraction</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="undefined-5">Day 6 — Golden Pavilion, Nishiki Market, Gion at Dusk</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Kinkaku-ji (Golden Pavilion)</strong> (morning) — there is no quiet time here; arrive at opening and accept the crowds. The reflection in the pond is worth it regardless.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Nishiki Market</strong> (lunch) — a narrow covered street a few blocks long, packed with vendors selling pickled vegetables, grilled skewers, tofu, matcha sweets. Go hungry.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Gion district</strong> (6–8pm) — when Kyoto looks most like the image people have of it. Hanamikoji street, lantern-lit machiya townhouses, the occasional geiko moving through the backstreets. It's atmospheric at this hour in a way it isn't at noon.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:image {"id":1703,"sizeSlug":"large","linkDestination":"none"} -->

<!-- /wp:image -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A <strong>tea ceremony</strong> is one of the most worthwhile bookings in Kyoto. Prices vary by setting and format, but a good session usually includes prepared matcha, traditional sweets, and instruction. It sounds like a tourist checkbox. It is not. Book in advance because good morning slots can go quickly.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><a href="https://www.viator.com/tours/Kyoto/Experience-Tea-Ceremony-wearing-a-Kimono-in-Kyoto/d332-51440P61?pid=P00300034&amp;mcid=42383&amp;medium=link&amp;campaign=japan-10day" rel="sponsored noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Book the Kimono Tea Ceremony at Kyoto Maikoya on Viator →</a> — currently rated 4.85 from over 1,000 reviews, and the kimono is included. Morning slots sell out weeks ahead in peak season; check the current rating before booking.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="undefined-6">Day 7 — Fushimi Inari at Dawn, Nara by Afternoon</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:group {"style":{"color":{"background":"#F0F4F8"},"spacing":{"padding":{"top":"20px","bottom":"20px","left":"24px","right":"24px"}},"border":{"left":{"color":"#5B8DB8","width":"4px"}}}} -->
<div class="wp-block-group has-background" style="border-left-color:#5B8DB8;border-left-width:4px;background-color:#F0F4F8;padding-top:20px;padding-right:24px;padding-bottom:20px;padding-left:24px"><!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Reality check:</strong> Fushimi Inari before 8am and Fushimi Inari at 11am are not the same place. The gate route that appears in every Japan photo exists at 7am. By mid-morning it's a crowded trail. Set one alarm and go.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph --></div>
<!-- /wp:group -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Fushimi Inari Taisha</strong> — arrive by 6:30am; the lower sections are quiet before 8am, and beyond the first 20 minutes of trail (where most people stop), it stays quiet regardless of hour</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Full hike</strong> — 2.5 miles (4km) round trip, 2–3 hours; the best concentration of gates is in the first third, but going further gives you mountain silence and shrine after shrine with no queue</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Nara</strong> (afternoon) - about 45 minutes from Kyoto by express train; free-roaming deer at Nara Park, Todai-ji temple, and a compact temple route. Half a day is enough for most first-time visitors.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="osaka">Days 8–10 in Osaka: Street Food, Energy, and the Last Nights</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Osaka is Japan's food city — and the contrast with Kyoto is immediate. Louder, less refined, more fun at street level. The density of good, affordable eating in the Dotonbori and Namba area is unlike anywhere else on this itinerary. It's also the cheapest of the four stops.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Getting there from Kyoto: 15 minutes on the shinkansen.</strong> You can return to Kyoto from Osaka for anything you missed — it's a realistic day trip if needed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- /wp:image -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="undefined-7">Day 8 — Dotonbori, Shinsaibashi, First Night Out</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Dotonbori</strong> — the canal, the neon, the giant mechanical crab. Walk it slowly and eat from the stalls. Try takoyaki (grilled octopus balls) from a street vendor, not a sit-down restaurant.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Shinsaibashi covered arcade</strong> — one of the longest covered shopping streets in Japan; useful for souvenirs and everything else without weather concerns</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Evening food tour</strong> — the highest-value option for a first night in Osaka. A good guide takes you through 5–6 spots in 3 hours — local izakayas, stalls, places you wouldn't find walking past. You eat more variety than you would on your own.</li>

<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="day-9-osaka-castle-and-open-agenda">Day 9 — Osaka Castle and Open Agenda</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Osaka Castle</strong> (morning) - the park grounds are free; the castle exterior is the highlight. The interior museum has a separate ticket and is useful if you want the history, skippable if you mostly came for the view and photos.</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li><strong>Flexibility options:</strong> back to Kyoto for a temple you missed (15 min away), Universal Studios Japan (20 min by direct train, book tickets in advance for peak season), or simply slow down and eat your way through Osaka's covered arcades</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="day-10-departure-from-kansai-airport-kix">Day 10 — Departure from Kansai Airport (KIX)</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Kansai International Airport is <strong>70 minutes from central Osaka</strong> by direct Haruka express. Allow more time than you'd expect — KIX is a full international airport and check-in takes longer than a domestic flight. Most morning departures mean leaving the hotel by 5–6am.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>If your return flight is from Tokyo rather than Osaka, take the last shinkansen from Osaka the evening before — 2.5 hours, gives you a final night in Tokyo before a morning departure. Flying home from Osaka is simpler for this itinerary; worth considering when booking flights.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="seven-days">If You Only Have 7 Days</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:table {"hasFixedLayout":false} -->
<figure class="wp-block-table"><table><thead><tr><th>Days</th><th>Location</th><th>Nights</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1–2</td><td>Tokyo</td><td>2</td></tr><tr><td>3–5</td><td>Kyoto</td><td>3</td></tr><tr><td>6–7</td><td>Osaka</td><td>1 (fly home Day 7)</td></tr></tbody></table></figure>
<!-- /wp:table -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Seven days works, but the trade-off is real: you lose Hakone and the ryokan night. If forced to choose, the better move is cutting Tokyo to 2 nights rather than removing Hakone entirely. A third day in Tokyo is replaceable. The ryokan isn't.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="worth-it">Is 10 Days in Japan Enough?</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>For a first visit: yes. Tokyo, Hakone, Kyoto, and Osaka cover the four destinations that consistently come up as the highlights of Japan for first-time visitors. You'll leave with a clear picture of what the country is — and a list of specific things to return for, which is exactly the right outcome.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>What you're not covering:</strong> Hiroshima and Miyajima (add these if you have 14 days), Kanazawa (one of Japan's most underrated cities), the Japanese Alps, the north. All valid reasons for a second trip — and Japan is one of the destinations where a second trip is genuinely common.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The 14-hour flight is the hardest part. Japan itself is surprisingly easy to navigate independently. The trains run on time, signage is reliable in English, and the food is exceptional at every price point. The effort-to-reward ratio is one of the best in international travel.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="what-id-change">What First-Time Japan Visitors Usually Get Wrong</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Over-scheduling Tokyo.</strong> Three nights feels generous until you realize basic navigation and jet lag eat the first day. Most visitors either feel rushed in Tokyo or compensate by shortchanging Kyoto — which is where the trip tends to become unforgettable. Three nights, no more, no less.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Treating Hakone as a day trip.</strong> The ropeway and Lake Ashi are fine. The ryokan is the reason to go. Doing Hakone without staying overnight is like going to the Amalfi Coast and not eating dinner on the water - you have technically been there, but you have missed the best part. If the budget allows, the overnight adds much more to the trip than another standard city hotel night.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Skipping the early morning at Fushimi Inari.</strong> One alarm, one morning. The whole memory of Kyoto changes.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>Assuming the JR Pass automatically saves money.</strong> It does not. There is no 10-day nationwide pass, and the 7-day or 14-day pass needs to be compared against your exact trains. Calculate before buying, especially if your route is one-way from Tokyo to Osaka.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->

<p>One to avoid: July and August. Tokyo and Osaka in midsummer run 90–95°F (32–35°C) with high humidity. It's not impossible, but it's genuinely uncomfortable for a trip built around walking.</p>

<p>Japan consistently surprises people who expect it to be difficult. The trains run on time. Signage works. The food is exceptional at every price point. The general experience of being somewhere where things work reliably is its own kind of travel luxury — and it's available at the mid-range, not just at the top.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="faq">Japan 10-Day Itinerary: Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="is-10-days-enough-for-japan">Is 10 days enough for Japan?</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Yes, for a first visit — if the itinerary stays focused on three to four cities. This plan covers Tokyo, Hakone, Kyoto, and Osaka, which are consistently the highlights of Japan for first-time visitors. A route that adds Hiroshima, Kanazawa, or the Japanese Alps is better suited to 14 days.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="how-much-does-a-10-day-japan-trip-cost">How much does a 10-day Japan trip cost?</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A comfortable 10-day Japan trip can vary widely depending on flights, hotel season, ryokan choice, and how many paid activities you add. The safest approach is to price the big pieces separately: flights, hotels, long-distance trains, Hakone ryokan, food, and activities. Japan can be excellent value at the mid-range, but the JR Pass should be calculated rather than assumed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="do-i-need-a-jr-pass-for-10-days-in-japan">Do I need a JR Pass for 10 days in Japan?</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Not automatically. There is no 10-day nationwide JR Pass; the official nationwide pass comes in 7, 14, and 21-day versions. For a Tokyo - Hakone - Kyoto - Osaka route, compare individual tickets with the current 7-day or 14-day pass price. A pass may make sense if you add a return to Tokyo or extra long-distance JR day trips, but it is not something to assume for this one-way itinerary.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="whats-the-best-area-to-stay-in-tokyo">What's the best area to stay in Tokyo?</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Shinjuku or Shibuya for this itinerary. Both have direct subway and JR connections to every part of the city and shinkansen access for the Hakone leg. Shinjuku has slightly better mid-range hotel value; Shibuya is marginally more central for the western sights. Either works.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="what-should-i-book-in-advance-for-japan">What should I book in advance for Japan?</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Hakone ryokans (2–3 months ahead), Kyoto tea ceremonies, and early Fushimi Inari tours. Universal Studios Japan tickets require advance booking in peak season. Hotels and flights: 3–4 months out, especially for cherry blossom (late March to mid-April) and autumn foliage (mid-October to mid-November).</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="what-is-the-best-time-of-year-to-visit-japan">What is the best time of year to visit Japan?</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>May and September-October are usually the easiest months for a first Japan trip: comfortable sightseeing weather, strong scenery, and less pressure than peak sakura dates. Cherry blossom and autumn foliage are spectacular but require earlier booking. July-August can be hot and humid in Tokyo, Kyoto, and Osaka; winter is quieter and can be excellent if you pack for cold mornings.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
